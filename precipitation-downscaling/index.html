Project Overview

This project tests whether precipitation downscaling can give finer spatial and temporal rainfall detail for the Indonesian application. We adapted two models, SpateGAN and CEDDAR, to IFS forecasts and evaluated them against Korean radar data.

Korean validation is complete for the reported configurations. Both models improve some error measures over IFS, but both have lower rainfall detection and spatial agreement scores. Example runs show that the workflow also runs over Indonesia, the Philippines, and Ghana. Accuracy in those regions has not been measured.

Model Approaches

SpateGAN

SpateGAN is a generative adversarial network that increases the spatial and temporal resolution of rainfall fields. A generator produces detailed rainfall sequences, and a discriminator helps it learn realistic rainfall patterns. The original model takes hourly, 24 km ERA5 precipitation and produces 10-minute, 2 km fields. It was trained on German radar observations. It can generate several rainfall realizations to represent uncertainty [1].

For this project, we adapted the pretrained model to IFS total precipitation and trained it further on Korean PEAKHSP targets. Plots and tables use these short names:

  • SpateGAN-IFS: the pretrained generator adapted to one IFS total-precipitation channel, without Korean fine-tuning.
  • SpateGAN-PEAK*: the PEAK checkpoint at step 500. This is the selected checkpoint.
  • SpateGAN-KMA: the comparison checkpoint at step 1,000.
  • SpateGAN-PEAK-5k: the PEAK training continued to step 5,000, to check whether longer training helps.
  • CEDDAR-PEAK: the selected CEDDAR checkpoint, B0 configuration at epoch 19.

A selected checkpoint is the one the project chose under its recorded criteria. It is not necessarily the best on every metric, and it is not a claim that SpateGAN is better than CEDDAR.

CEDDAR

CEDDAR is a diffusion model. It generates high-resolution rainfall by repeated denoising, guided by coarse-resolution precipitation. Each sample is one possible rainfall field, so several samples form an ensemble. The original model downscales daily ERA5 precipitation from about 30 km to 2.5 km over Denmark. Its reference data is the DANRA regional reanalysis [2].

For this project, we adapted CEDDAR to paired IFS and PEAKHSP data and trained the B0 configuration from scratch. The rainfall evaluation over all 1,518 forecasts is complete. The results below come from that evaluation, not from training or validation loss.

CEDDAR generates separate three-hour rainfall fields. It does not learn a continuous rainfall sequence. Consecutive outputs can be shown together to follow an event over time. The reported results use the corrected PEAK B0 checkpoint at epoch 19 (CEDDAR-PEAK), with three ensemble members and 56 denoising steps.

Data and Evaluation Design

The development dataset covers February 2024 to December 2025 over Korea.

IFS provides the forecast rainfall inputs. PEAKHSP rainfall, prepared from KMA radar data, provides the high-resolution targets over Korea. The PEAK paper is under review. DI Cast 2 km, a satellite rainfall product, is included as an extra comparison. Performance in Indonesia has not yet been assessed.

  • IFS: ECMWF Integrated Forecasting System forecasts [3]. Total precipitation is prepared as three-hour amounts on a 24 km grid. The selected input sequences keep their forecast cycle and lead times.
  • PEAKHSP: Korean radar rainfall at 2 km spatial resolution, integrated into half-hour amounts. For CEDDAR, these are summed into three-hour amounts.
  • DI Cast 2 km: satellite-derived rainfall, aligned to the regional analysis grids for comparison. How to interpret the product is still provisional. Missing observations stay visible, and DI Cast over Indonesia is not treated as ground truth.

Model-specific Preparation

IFS and PEAKHSP data are paired by location and rainfall interval, then prepared in the model-specific formats described above. Rainfall amounts are stored in millimetres. Timestamps use UTC and mark the end of each rainfall interval. The current PEAK datasets keep complete targets and do not impute missing values. Normalization statistics use training data only. Checks for leakage between the training and validation splits passed.

The models use different sample formats, so the sample counts are not directly comparable. An independent test set is still needed to assess performance after model development.

Validation blocks

Both datasets use records from February 1, 2024 to December 31, 2025. Each block starts and ends at 00:00 UTC; the start is included and the end is not. Training uses the remaining eligible records, after removing windows and cycles that overlap a validation block. SpateGAN also excludes samples that share IFS or target times with validation. CEDDAR also excludes other samples from cycles that provide validation targets.

Korean Validation Results

The selected SpateGAN checkpoint has lower MAE and CRPS than its matched IFS reference. However, it underestimates rainfall and has lower detection and spatial scores. CEDDAR has lower CRPS than IFS, but higher MAE and lower detection and spatial scores. These results support further evaluation. They do not show a general accuracy improvement over IFS.

Results by rainfall threshold

The thresholds are 0.1, 1, 3, 5 and 8 mm/hour, converted to three-hour amounts. Counts and sums are pooled over all evaluated samples before scores are calculated. POD-M, FAR-M and CSI-M are the equal-weight mean of the defined scores at the five thresholds. Undefined scores stay blank and are left out; they are not set to zero.

Ensemble FSS, threshold against spatial scale

Ensemble FSS is the unweighted mean over 35 combinations: five thresholds by seven neighbourhood sizes (1, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128 km). The 1 km label stands for one 2 km grid cell. Only the mean is recorded so far. The breakdown by threshold and scale is not yet complete.

Understanding the metrics

Arrows show whether higher or lower values are better. M marks the unweighted mean across the rainfall thresholds. MAE is already an average error in rainfall amount. Both tables use unadjusted rainfall. FSS uses the saved ensemble-fraction calculation, not the legacy meFSS or the ensemble-mean FSS.

MetricMeaning
MAEAverage absolute error in rainfall amount.
PODFraction of observed rainfall events detected.
FARFraction of predicted rainfall events that were false alarms.
CSIDetection accuracy that counts hits, misses, and false alarms.
Ensemble FSSAgreement of ensemble rainfall-exceedance fractions with observed fractions across evaluation neighbourhood sizes. Higher is better.
CRPSError of the ensemble forecast distribution against the observed amount. Lower is better. For deterministic IFS, CRPS equals MAE.

MAE and detection scores use the ensemble mean. CRPS uses all three members. Results from three members do not establish calibration.

Computational cost

Training

Each run was allocated four CPU cores and 44 GiB of host memory. Times cover the full training runs, including validation and saving outputs. The selected checkpoints are step 500 and epoch 19. SpateGAN adapts the input layers of a pretrained model, while CEDDAR trains from scratch, so the two costs cover different amounts of training. Peak GPU memory and FLOPs during training were not measured.

Inference

Measured on one NVIDIA V100-SXM2 (32 GB) after warm-up, with three ensemble members and three timed repetitions across the 12 selected Korean and Indonesian cases. Times are mean plus or minus standard deviation. They exclude input preparation, transfers, file writing and visualization. One SpateGAN sample produces 48 half-hour fields over 24 hours. One CEDDAR sample produces one three-hour field, so a 24-hour case needs eight predictions. The output areas also differ. These are costs of the current configurations, not a matched comparison of the architectures.

Implications and Next Steps

Findings

SpateGAN-PEAK has lower three-hour MAE and CRPS than its matched IFS reference. However, it has lower CSI-M and Ensemble FSS, and produces about 28.2% less rain than observed. Its lower amount error comes with underestimation and weaker detection and spatial scores.

CEDDAR completed 400 training epochs, with its lowest validation loss at epoch 19. In the full rainfall evaluation, its MAE and its detection and spatial scores are worse than its matched IFS reference, but its CRPS is lower. Its total rainfall is about 5.1% above the target.

Continuing SpateGAN training to 5,000 steps did not improve it enough to replace step 500. Its total rainfall falls to 41% of the observed total, and its detection and spatial scores drop sharply. MAE alone would have suggested a small gain.

Application value and evidence required

The current evidence shows higher spatial and temporal resolution, but not consistently higher accuracy. Further evaluation should test whether the extra detail helps a defined application, such as finding local rainfall patterns or supporting downstream analysis.

Measuring performance in Indonesia needs suitable reference observations. Before choosing an evaluation dataset, assess radar and gauge coverage, data quality, access conditions, and cost. The Philippine radar cases are a first chance to test transfer to a tropical region, after coverage and quality checks. They cannot show performance in Indonesia.

Compute, storage, and data requirements

The reported training runs took 2.54 GPU-hours for SpateGAN and 10.49 GPU-hours for CEDDAR on one V100. These numbers apply to the current configurations only. They do not estimate the resources for broader model development, larger ensembles, or operational delivery.

Infrastructure priority. Reliable GPU access, enough storage, and enough data-transfer capacity would support repeated experiments, preprocessing, ensemble evaluation, and keeping inputs and results for reproducibility. Short single training runs do not show queue delays or the total work of comparing configurations. Size GPU concurrency, storage, and throughput from the planned experiment schedule and measured data volumes. This report does not yet quantify these requirements.

Data priority. The current training archive covers February 2024 to December 2025. Extending the Korean reference archive would add seasonal and event diversity and support a separate test period. Getting suitable observations in the application regions is equally important. More compute cannot show regional accuracy without reference data.

Development priorities

  • Independent evaluation: reserve a test period separate from model selection, complete the threshold-by-scale spatial analysis, and evaluate the available Philippine radar cases.
  • Additional predictors: test humidity, CAPE, wind, land-sea mask, and orography through controlled comparisons. Their contribution and resource requirements remain to be measured.
  • Supplementary targets: assess whether DI Cast can support fine-tuning where radar is absent. Its uncertainty would propagate into training, so independent observations would still be needed for validation.

Next steps

The immediate priorities are to reserve an independent test period, complete the Ensemble FSS breakdown by threshold and spatial scale, and assess applicability to Indonesia.

Forecast input. IFS availability limits the Indonesian application. GFS is being considered as a substitute or supplement, and other forecast sources are also being explored. GFS is downloaded at three-hour intervals, so its download interval alone does not require changes to the models’ time structure. Before reuse, check the precipitation accumulation windows, units, spatial grid and preprocessing. Then evaluate whether the models need fine-tuning for the new forecast source. GFS-based performance must be assessed separately from the IFS-based results on this page.

Cross-project reuse. The same pipeline has now run over the Philippines and Ghana. The section above shows what it produced and what it does not establish.
